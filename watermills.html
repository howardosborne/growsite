Watermills and landscape

The Ouse Valley Way passes through Houghton Mill, which owned by the National Trust and a popular tourist spot.

Here I met up with Keith Grimwade and Bridget Flannagan who have been digging into and researching the history of milling in the area and made some surprising findings.

Here is our conversation broken down into bite-size chunks that you can download and listen to while walking through Houghton Mill:

**add a map in here with the locations of the recordings

1 Introduction

We're not slow workers really, it was just a huge subject - how Keith and Bridget got started

2 Why is this place special?

What did the glaciers ever do for us? Keith explains how events long ago made watermills here the highest value at Domesday.

3 Who built the first local watermill?

Was it the Romans, Anglo-Saxons or even the Danes that build the first watermills here?

4 The location of the original Houghton Mill

Keith and Bridget make the case for where the original Houghton Mill was located.

5 How to find a missing watermill

Making things fom bio-degradable materials is seen as a good thing today, but it's something of a headache in archeology. Keith and Bridget explain how they found missing mills using some ingeneous ideas.

6 Innovation, expansion and aggrevation

The 1200s was a time of population expansion and an indreasing need for milling with new innovations in wind power but also tensions on the river.

7 Moving Heaven and earth

The Abbey carries out major engineering works to create new channels and new mills for grain and fulling.

8 Drawing places - compromise at a cost

The Abbey finds a way to keep the mills running and allow river traffic to pass through.

9 The arrival of locks

James I needed money and had a plan to expand trade.

10 Full steam ahead

Steam replaces water as the new energy source and one beneficiary uses new wealth to power new religious crusade

11 The rise of speculation and the decline of navigation

New owners try to renew river use and a means of transport, but meets with resistance.

12 A powerhouse of watermilling

Today's serene landscape belies how it would appear to our ancestors. Keith and Bridget have shown what it would have looked like and make the case for reevaluating what we should preserve.

Their book, The Watermills and Landscape of the River Great Ouse, Cambridgeshire, is available for sale at various bookstores including here.
